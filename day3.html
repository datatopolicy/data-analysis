Session 3.209:30–10:30 · 60 minutesPlenary

IMRAD structure

IMRAD — Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion — is the standard structure for the report each team will draft this week and present on Friday. This session teaches what belongs in each section and, critically, what must not cross between them.

Learning objectives

  1. State the purpose of each IMRAD section and the question it answers for the reader.
  2. Distinguish results from discussion, and keep interpretation out of the results section.
  3. Map their own team's work so far onto the structure, identifying what is ready and what is missing.

Detailed content

The four sections, and the question each answers (30 min)

SectionQuestion it answersWhat goes wrong most often
IntroductionWhy does this analysis matter, and what decision does it inform?Too broad — restates the whole health system instead of the specific decision
MethodsWhat data, what period, what calculations, what denominator?Missing the denominator source, so results cannot be reproduced or trusted
ResultsWhat did the data show, in plain language, with no interpretation?Interpretation leaks in — "worryingly," "due to," "because of"
DiscussionWhat does this mean in context, and what should happen next?Claims a cause the descriptive data cannot establish

The discipline that matters most: results versus discussion (20 min)

Read aloud a sentence that mixes the two: "Alarmingly, District A had the highest rate, probably due to weak facility access." Ask the room to identify what belongs in results ("District A had the highest rate, at X per 1,000") and what belongs in discussion, marked explicitly as a plausible explanation rather than a proven one ("this may be consistent with facility access; these data cannot establish the cause"). This exact discipline governs the writing work in Session 4.3 on Thursday.

Mapping the team's own work onto the structure (10 min)

Each team places its work so far onto the four sections: their Session 1.4 objective becomes the seed of the introduction; their Session 2.3 denominator citation and Session 1.5–1.7 cleaning steps become the methods; their Session 2.7 results table becomes the results section; and discussion remains open, to be drafted once more results are in hand.

Exercise 3.2: Map your report so far

Country teams · 20 minutes, folded into the session

  1. Write one sentence for your introduction, based on your Session 1.4 objective and the decision it serves.
  2. List the methods elements you already have: data source, period, cleaning steps, denominator citation.
  3. Paste in your Session 2.7 results table as a placeholder results section.
  4. Leave discussion blank for now, but write one open question you expect it will need to address.

Facilitator notes

Timing
The results-versus-discussion discussion is worth protecting even under time pressure — cut the mapping exercise short before cutting this.
Common pitfall
Teams treat the introduction as background reading on the whole disease rather than the specific decision their analysis serves. Redirect to the Session 1.4 objective every time.

Required datasets, tools and resources

imrad_structure_guide.pdfOne page per section: purpose, contents, common faults.
national_report_template.docxIntroduced here, used for drafting from Thursday onward.

Key takeaways and assessment checks

  • Results state what the data show; discussion interprets it.
  • A plausible explanation in discussion must be marked as plausible, not proven.
  1. Rewrite "alarmingly, District A had the highest rate, probably due to weak facility access" as a results sentence and a separate discussion sentence.

Session 3.311:00–12:00 · 60 minutesPlenary

Best practices for creating tables

A table should be interpretable on its own, without the surrounding text. This session teaches the components a publication-ready table needs and the formatting choices that make or break its readability.

Learning objectives

  1. Name the components a table needs to stand alone: title, headers, units, source, and notes.
  2. Apply consistent number formatting and appropriate row ordering.
  3. Identify and fix the most common faults in a poorly built table.

Detailed content

The components of a standalone table (25 min)

Table 1. Confirmed cases and rate per 1,000 population by district
and sex, [Country], 2024                                  <- TITLE

District   | Male cases | Male rate | Female cases | Female rate  <- HEADERS
-----------------------------------------------------------------
District A |        142 |      41.2 |          118 |         33.1
District B |         98 |      29.4 |           87 |         25.2

Rates per 1,000 population.                               <- UNITS
Source: [dataset], extract [date]. Denominator: [projection, table, year]. <- SOURCE
Note: districts below 80% reporting completeness are shaded.  <- NOTES

Test every table against one question: if a reader saw only this page, could they interpret it correctly and know where it came from? If not, a component is missing.

Number formatting and row ordering (20 min)

PRECISION   Round to what the data actually supports — one or two
            decimal places for a rate is usually enough; never
            round a count.
ALIGNMENT   Numbers right-aligned or decimal-aligned; never centered.
CONSISTENCY Same number of decimals down a column, including whole
            numbers: 38.7, 29.0, not 38.7, 29.
ORDERING    Order rows by the value the table is about — usually
            descending — not alphabetically, unless a reader genuinely
            needs to look up their own district by name.

Fixing a bad table together (15 min)

Project a deliberately poor table missing several of these components and have the room call out what is wrong before revealing the fixed version, setting up the tables exercise that follows immediately after this session.

Exercise 3.3: Diagnose the bad table

Plenary, called out from the floor · 15 minutes within the session

  1. Working from the projected poor table, call out every missing or wrong component: title, units, source, notes, alignment, ordering.
  2. Facilitator reveals the corrected version and confirms which faults were caught.

Facilitator notes

Timing
Keep the diagnosis exercise brisk — it is a warm-up for the full tables exercise in the next session, not the main event.
Common pitfall
Participants add heavy borders and color to make a table "look professional," which reduces legibility. Teach the minimum: a rule above headers, a rule below them, nothing else.

Required datasets, tools and resources

table_best_practices_guide.pdfComponents, formatting rules, and the projected bad-table example.

Key takeaways and assessment checks

  • A table must be interpretable on its own: title, units, source and notes are not optional.
  • Order rows to support the comparison, not the alphabet.
  1. Name the five components a standalone table needs.

Session 3.412:00–13:00 · 60 minutesApplied practice

Tables exercise

Applying this morning's table standards directly to each team's own results table from Tuesday, turning a working spreadsheet output into a report-ready table.

Learning objectives

  1. Rebuild a working results table into a full, standalone, report-ready table.
  2. Apply consistent number formatting and appropriate ordering to real data.

Detailed content

From working table to report table (10 min briefing)

A working table built during analysis is optimized for the analyst's own understanding. A report table is optimized for a reader who has never seen the underlying spreadsheet. This exercise makes that conversion explicit: teams take their Session 2.7 results table and rebuild it with every component from this morning's session present.

Exercise 3.4: Rebuild your results table

Country teams · full hour

  1. Start from your Session 2.7 results table.
  2. Write a complete title stating what, who, where and when.
  3. Add units to every numeric column header.
  4. Apply consistent decimal formatting throughout.
  5. Order rows by the value the table is about.
  6. Add the source line with your exact denominator citation from Session 2.3, and any relevant notes (for example, on reporting completeness).
  7. Exchange with another team for a two-minute check: can they interpret it without asking you anything?

Facilitator notes

Timing
The cross-team check in step 7 is the single most valuable five minutes — protect it even if earlier steps run long.
Common pitfall
Teams write a title that names the topic but not the geography or year ("Malaria cases by district"). Push for all four elements: what, who, where, when.
Real-world edge case
A team's data has known reporting gaps in one district. Teach them to note this directly in the table rather than silently including a potentially misleading number.

Required datasets, tools and resources

Team's own Session 2.7 results tableThe working table to be rebuilt.
table_best_practices_guide.pdfComponent checklist from Session 3.3.

Key takeaways and assessment checks

  • A report table and a working table serve different purposes and look different.
  • A reader who has never seen your spreadsheet should be able to interpret your table unaided.
  1. What is the difference in purpose between a working table and a report table?

Session 3.514:00–15:00 · 60 minutesPlenary

Best practices for creating figures

Excel's default chart settings are built for business dashboards, not epidemiology, and a chart left at its defaults is often harder to read than the table behind it. This session teaches which chart type answers which question, and the small set of default settings that must always be changed.

Learning objectives

  1. Choose the chart type that matches the comparison being made.
  2. Apply the core default-repair list: titles, axis starting point, gridlines, and legends.
  3. Identify when a chart should not be used at all — a table or a sentence would serve better.

Detailed content

Matching chart to question (25 min)

QuestionChartNot this
How did cases change over time?Line chart, time on the horizontal axisA pie chart, or bars across a long series
Which districts have the highest rate?Horizontal bars, sorted descendingA pie chart, or bars in alphabetical order
What share of cases came from each cause?Sorted horizontal barsA pie chart beyond about four categories

State two firm rules: no three-dimensional charts, because the perspective distorts the very comparison the chart exists to show; and no pie charts beyond three or four categories, because angles are harder to compare accurately than bar lengths.

The default-repair list (25 min)

1. Chart title      Replace "Chart Title" with a sentence stating the
                     finding: "Confirmed cases fell in three of four
                     districts, 2021-2024" — not just "Cases by district."
2. Axis titles       Add both, with units: "Cases per 1,000 population."
3. Axis start        For bar charts, the vertical axis must start at
                     zero, always. A truncated axis exaggerates the
                     apparent difference between bars.
4. Gridlines          Delete vertical gridlines; keep light horizontal
                      ones only if exact values must be read off.
5. Legend             Delete it for a single series. For two or three,
                      label lines directly rather than using a legend box.

When not to use a chart at all (10 min)

A single comparison between two numbers is a sentence, not a chart. A table with more than about six rows a reader needs to look up individually is often clearer as a table than a chart. Teach participants to ask, before building any chart, whether it will show something a sentence or a table could not.

Exercise 3.5: Diagnose three bad charts

Plenary, called out from the floor · 15 minutes within the session

  1. For each of three projected charts — a pie chart of nine causes, a line chart with a truncated axis, and a default-title bar chart — identify the single worst fault and what a reader would wrongly conclude as a result.

Facilitator notes

Timing
The 14:30–15:00 window continues this same content as one uninterrupted hour, moving into the hands-on figures exercise that follows.
Common pitfall
A supervisor or minister's office may prefer a pie chart regardless of the guidance here. Teach the response: produce the recommended chart and the requested one side by side, and let the recommended version's clarity make the argument.

Required datasets, tools and resources

figure_best_practices_guide.pdfChart-type matching table and the default-repair checklist.
bad_charts_examples.xlsxThree seeded-fault charts for the diagnosis exercise.

Key takeaways and assessment checks

  • Bar charts start at zero, always.
  • Chart titles state findings, not variable names.
  • Not every comparison needs a chart — sometimes a sentence is clearer.
  1. A bar chart's vertical axis starts at 38 rather than 0. What effect does this have, and what do you do about it?

Session 3.615:00–16:00 · 60 minutesApplied practice

Figures exercise

Building one publication-ready figure from each team's own results table, applying this afternoon's chart-selection rule and default-repair list directly.

Learning objectives

  1. Choose the appropriate chart type for their own team's key comparison.
  2. Build the chart in Excel and apply the full default-repair list.

Detailed content

From table to figure (10 min briefing)

Not every table needs a matching figure. Teach teams to choose one genuinely important comparison from their Session 3.4 table — usually the one their objective is really about — and build a single figure for it, rather than mechanically charting every row of every table.

Exercise 3.6: Build your key figure

Country teams · full hour

  1. Choose the one comparison from your table that most directly answers your Session 1.4 objective.
  2. Select the appropriate chart type using this afternoon's matching table.
  3. Build it in Excel and apply the full default-repair list: title stating the finding, axis titles with units, axis starting at zero for bars, minimal gridlines, no unnecessary legend.
  4. Exchange with another team for a check: does the title state a finding, and would a reader understand the chart without your table?

Facilitator notes

Timing
Circulate and check specifically for the axis-start rule on bar charts — it is the single most common fault this exercise produces.
Common pitfall
A finding-based title comes out as a label ("Cases by district"). Push for the sentence version every time — if a team cannot write one, the figure may not have a clear finding yet.

Required datasets, tools and resources

Team's own Session 3.4 report tableSource data for the figure.
figure_best_practices_guide.pdfChart-selection table and default-repair checklist.

Key takeaways and assessment checks

  • Build one figure for the comparison that matters most, not one for every row of data.
  1. How do you decide which single comparison from your table deserves a figure?

Session 3.716:00–17:00 · 60 minutesDemonstration then applied practice

Creating maps in Excel

A district-level map is often the most persuasive way to show a geographic pattern, and it does not require GIS software. This session teaches a defensible Excel-based mapping approach and the interpretation traps specific to maps.

Learning objectives

  1. Build a district-level filled map in Excel from rate data.
  2. State why a map should show rates rather than counts.
  3. Recognize when a sorted bar chart communicates a ranking more clearly than a map.

Detailed content

Building a filled map (25 min)

Excel Filled Map (2016+, requires internet at creation time)
  Two columns: district name, rate value.
  Insert > Maps > Filled Map
  District names must match the service's recognized names — verify
  every district actually appears shaded before trusting the map;
  it drops unmatched names silently rather than raising an error.

Where Filled Map is unavailable or unreliable for a country's district names, the fallback is a sorted horizontal bar chart, which communicates a ranking at least as clearly and requires no external service.

Map rates, not counts (15 min)

A map of counts makes populous districts look worse regardless of underlying risk, because they naturally accumulate more events. Always map the rate — and where a district's population is very small, its rate can be extreme from very few events; note this in the map's caption rather than letting one small district visually dominate the map.

When a bar chart beats a map (10 min)

A map is the right choice when the spatial pattern itself is the finding — clustering near a border, for instance. When the finding is really a ranking, a sorted bar chart is usually clearer, because readers judge bar length more accurately than map color. Ask each team to decide, for their own data, which is the better choice, and be prepared to justify either answer.

Exercise 3.7: Build your map or bar chart

Country teams, applied practice · remainder of the hour

  1. Using your team's district-level rate table, attempt a filled map in Excel.
  2. Verify every district is shaded; if any are missing, note the naming mismatch.
  3. Build the equivalent sorted horizontal bar chart of the same data.
  4. Decide which you will use in your report and write one sentence justifying the choice.

Facilitator notes

Timing
Do not let the Filled Map's naming quirks consume the whole hour — set a ten-minute limit on troubleshooting before a team moves to the bar-chart fallback.
Common pitfall
A team maps counts rather than rates because the map "looks fuller." Ask what the map would look like if the darkest district had half its current population, to make the point concrete.
Real-world edge case
Excel's Filled Map does not recognize a country's district names correctly, and several districts remain unshaded. This is common; do not treat it as a personal error. Move to the bar-chart alternative and note the limitation.

Required datasets, tools and resources

Team's own district-level rate tableFrom Session 2.3 and 2.5.
maps_in_excel_guide.pdfFilled Map steps and the rates-not-counts rule.

Key takeaways and assessment checks

  • Map rates, not counts.
  • Use a map when the pattern is spatial; use a sorted bar chart when the finding is a ranking.
  1. Why does mapping counts rather than rates mislead a reader?
