Session 4.209:30–10:30 · 60 minutesPlenary

Guidelines for creating report templates

Beyond this week's single report, the project asks teams to develop templates for reporting on their priority diseases and conditions — a documented SOP that lets next quarter's report be produced faster than this one. This session teaches what makes a template reusable rather than a one-off document.

Learning objectives

  1. Explain the difference between a one-off report and a reusable report template.
  2. Identify the elements of this week's report that should be templated for reuse.
  3. Draft the skeleton of their own country's report template.

Detailed content

What makes a template, not just a document (20 min)

A one-off report has this quarter's numbers typed directly into the prose. A template separates the fixed structure — section headings, standard methods language, standard table and figure formats, the standard denominator citation format — from the variable content that changes each edition. The test: could a colleague who was not part of this week's analysis take next quarter's extract and produce the next edition using only the template and the data, without reconstructing decisions from scratch?

What to template from this week's work (20 min)

TEMPLATE THESE ELEMENTS
  - Section headings and their fixed purpose (from Session 3.2's IMRAD guide)
  - Standard methods paragraph structure, with blanks for period,
    extract date, and denominator citation
  - Table and figure formats built in Sessions 3.3-3.7, saved as a
    reusable Excel style
  - The standard limitations checklist (data completeness, denominator
    age, known cleaning decisions)

LEAVE VARIABLE
  - The actual numbers, tables and figures for each edition
  - The specific findings and recommendations, which change each time
  - Anything specific to a one-time event rather than the recurring report

Drafting the skeleton (20 min)

Each team starts its own country's reusable template directly from the national report template introduced in Session 3.2, marking which parts are fixed structure and which are this-edition content, using color or a comment to distinguish the two clearly for future users of the template.

Exercise 4.2: Mark up your template

Country teams · 20 minutes, folded into the session

  1. Open the national report template and, section by section, mark what is fixed structure versus this-edition content.
  2. Write the standard methods paragraph with blanks for the variable elements (period, extract date, denominator citation).
  3. Note where your team's own table and figure formats from Wednesday should be saved as the standard style.

Facilitator notes

Timing
This session sets up work that continues informally through the rest of the day as teams draft — do not expect a finished template by the end of the hour.
Common pitfall
Teams template too much, including specific findings that will not recur. Push back with the reuse test: would next quarter's report genuinely repeat this exact sentence?

Key takeaways and assessment checks

  • A template separates fixed structure from this-edition content.
  • Apply the reuse test: would the next edition genuinely repeat this element?
  1. Give one example each of something that belongs in the fixed template and something that must stay variable.

Session 4.311:00–12:00 · 60 minutesPlenary then applied practice

Drafting appropriate recommendations

Recommendations are where descriptive analysis most often overreaches into claims it cannot support, or collapses into vague advice nobody can act on. This session teaches recommendations that are both honest about what the data shows and specific enough to act on.

Learning objectives

  1. Write a recommendation that names an actor, an action, and preferably a timeframe.
  2. Avoid recommendations that imply a causal claim the descriptive analysis cannot support.
  3. Draft their own report's recommendations from their actual results.

Detailed content

Actor, action, timeframe (20 min)

POOR   "Surveillance should be strengthened."
POOR   "More research is needed."
GOOD   "District health teams in District A and District B should
        review case management capacity at the facilities reporting
        the highest rates, before the next quarterly review."
GOOD   "The statistics office should investigate the reporting gap
        in District C, where completeness fell below 60% in 2024."

Three to five recommendations, each naming someone who could read
the report and act on it. If nobody in the readership can act on a
recommendation, it does not belong in this report.

Staying inside what descriptive analysis can claim (20 min)

Reconnect to Wednesday's IMRAD discussion: a recommendation built on "X caused Y" when the analysis only showed "X and Y occurred together" oversteps the evidence and risks being challenged during clearance. Teach the safer construction: describe the pattern, note it is consistent with a plausible explanation, and recommend an action that would help regardless of which explanation turns out to be correct — often further investigation, a specific data quality fix, or a targeted service review.

Drafting from real results (20 min)

Teams draft their own three to five recommendations directly from their Session 2.7 results and Session 3.2 discussion notes, applying the actor-action-timeframe structure to real findings rather than a hypothetical example.

Exercise 4.3: Draft your recommendations

Country teams · 20 minutes, folded into the session

  1. From your results, list every recommendation currently under consideration.
  2. For each, name the specific actor and action; add a timeframe where reasonable.
  3. Check each recommendation for a hidden causal claim your data cannot support, and rephrase if needed.
  4. Cut any recommendation nobody in your intended readership could act on.

Facilitator notes

Timing
Circulate specifically to check for hidden causal claims — this is the fault that most often surfaces only when someone reads the recommendation aloud.
Common pitfall
Teams write recommendations addressed to no one in particular ("the government should..."). Push for the specific office or role that would actually receive and act on this report.
Real-world edge case
A team's only honest recommendation is about a data quality problem rather than the health condition itself. This is a legitimate and often valuable recommendation — do not discourage it in favor of a more dramatic-sounding but less supported one.

Required datasets, tools and resources

recommendations_drafting_guide.pdfActor-action-timeframe structure and the causal-claim check.
Team's own results and discussion notesFrom Sessions 2.7 and 3.2.

Key takeaways and assessment checks

  • Every recommendation names an actor and an action.
  • A recommendation should not rest on a causal claim the descriptive data cannot support.
  1. Turn "surveillance should be strengthened" into a specific, actionable recommendation, inventing reasonable details.

Applied practice: country teams draft reports

This block was not explicitly labeled in the source schedule; it is shown here as dedicated drafting time so that Thursday reads as a complete, workable day. Confirm or replace with your finalized agenda.

Learning objectives

  1. Advance the draft report across all four IMRAD sections using this morning's content.

Detailed content

How this hour is used (10 min briefing)

Teams work independently, applying the report template from Session 4.2 and the recommendations drafted in Session 4.3 to build out a continuous draft, rather than a set of disconnected fragments. Facilitators circulate and prioritize any team still missing more than one IMRAD section, identified in this morning's debrief.

Exercise 4.4: Continuous draft

Country teams · full hour

  1. Assemble your introduction, methods, results and discussion into one continuous document using the report template.
  2. Insert your recommendations from Session 4.3 at the end of the discussion.
  3. Read the whole draft aloud within your team, checking specifically for interpretation that has leaked into the results section.

Facilitator notes

Timing
Prioritize facilitator time on the teams flagged in this morning's debrief as having more than one blank section.
Common pitfall
Teams keep sections in separate files rather than assembling a continuous draft, which makes tomorrow's presentation prep harder. Insist on one continuous document by the end of this hour.

Session 4.514:00–14:30 · 30 minutesFacilitator-led team check-ins

Check-in on the progress of group projects

A structured, time-boxed check-in with every country team, ensuring each one has a concrete, achievable plan to reach a complete draft by the end of the afternoon.

Learning objectives

  1. Receive specific, prioritized feedback on the current draft from a facilitator.
  2. Leave the check-in with a concrete plan for the remaining afternoon.

Detailed content

The structure of the check-in (full session)

Each team gets roughly six to seven minutes with an assigned facilitator. The facilitator asks to see the current draft, checks specifically for: results and discussion kept separate (Session 3.2), every table and figure carrying its full set of components (Sessions 3.3–3.6), recommendations that name an actor and action (Session 4.3), and gives at most two priority fixes for the remaining time — not a comprehensive review, which would take longer than the check-in allows and would overwhelm the team this late in the week.

Exercise 4.5: The prioritized check-in

Country teams with an assigned facilitator · roughly 6–7 minutes each, run in parallel

  1. Present your current draft to your assigned facilitator.
  2. Receive at most two priority fixes.
  3. Write down a specific plan for the remaining afternoon, addressing those two fixes first.

Facilitator notes

Timing
Discipline is essential here — with four teams and thirty minutes, each facilitator has roughly six to seven minutes per team. Set a visible timer.
Common pitfall
Facilitators try to give comprehensive feedback rather than two priorities. Enforce the two-fix limit; a team this close to the deadline cannot usefully absorb more.
Real-world edge case
A team is well ahead of the others. Use their check-in time to push them toward a genuine stretch — for example, a second figure or a sharper discussion — rather than repeating feedback they have already addressed.

Required datasets, tools and resources

Each team's current draftFrom Session 4.4.

Key takeaways and assessment checks

  • A prioritized, two-fix check-in is more useful this late in the week than a comprehensive review.
  1. Why does this check-in deliberately limit feedback to two priority fixes?

Session 4.614:30–17:00 · 150 minutesApplied team practice

Finalizing report drafts

This block was not explicitly labeled in the source schedule; it is shown here as extended finalization time so Thursday reads as a complete, workable day. Confirm or replace with your finalized agenda. The goal: every team leaves today with a complete draft ready to become tomorrow's presentation.

Learning objectives

  1. Address the priority fixes identified in Session 4.5.
  2. Produce a complete draft report covering all four IMRAD sections.
  3. Begin identifying which results and figures will anchor tomorrow's presentation.

Detailed content

How the afternoon is structured (10 min briefing, then continuous work)

Teams work through their two priority fixes first, then continue polishing the full draft. In the final thirty minutes, ask each team to identify the three to four results or figures they consider strongest — this becomes the starting point for tomorrow's presentation template, so the choice is made deliberately today rather than rushed tomorrow morning.

Exercise 4.6: Complete and select

Country teams · full afternoon block

  1. Address your two priority fixes from Session 4.5 first.
  2. Complete all four IMRAD sections to a presentable standard.
  3. Finalize your recommendations using the actor-action-timeframe structure.
  4. In the final thirty minutes, select the three to four strongest results or figures for tomorrow's presentation, and note why each was chosen.
  5. Save your complete draft using the finalized report template.

Facilitator notes

Timing
Circulate continuously through the afternoon rather than waiting for a scheduled check-in; this is the last extended work block before Friday's presentations.
Common pitfall
Teams keep polishing prose and run out of time to select presentation content. Prompt the selection step explicitly with thirty minutes remaining if a team has not started it.
Real-world edge case
A team is not going to finish a full draft by 17:00 despite best efforts. Have them prioritize a complete results section and clear recommendations over a polished introduction — the results and recommendations are what tomorrow's audience most needs to see.

Key takeaways and assessment checks

  • By end of today, every team should have a complete draft and a deliberately chosen set of presentation highlights.
  • If time runs short, prioritize complete results and recommendations over a polished introduction.
  1. If your team cannot finish everything, which sections should you prioritize, and why?
